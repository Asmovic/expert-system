# Examiner-Style Review of the Report Draft

**Reviewed:** `report/main.pdf`, 86 pages, draft of 2026-10-21.
**Method:** every chapter was read as a sceptical examiner would read it, and the main claims were checked against the code, the generated results and a significance test run for this review.

**Overall assessment.** The report is complete against the framework's structure. It is unusually strong on reproducibility, honesty about weaknesses, and traceability from literature to rules. Its main exposure is the validity of the evaluation: the test data come from a simulator built on the same model as the knowledge base, the comparison between reasoning modes is over-interpreted, and two headline metrics are close to true by construction. None of this undermines the work, but each point needs to be addressed in the text before an examiner raises it.

Findings are grouped by severity. Each says what is wrong, why it matters, and what to do. Items marked **[I can do]** are ones I can carry out on request.

### A2. The differences between reasoning modes are mostly not significant

**Status: done.** Wilson intervals and McNemar tables are now generated by `tests/metrics_latex.pl` (Table `tab:significance-holdout`), and the abstract, Chapter 4, Chapter 6 and Chapter 7 have been reworded.

**What is wrong.** Chapter 4, the conclusion and the abstract compare the four configurations as if the differences between them were real. A paired exact McNemar test on the 255 held-out cases, run for this review, gives the following:

| Comparison | Cases only the first gets right | Cases only the second gets right | p |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deductive vs deductive + abductive | 2 | 11 | 0.022 |
| Deductive vs complete | 3 | 10 | 0.092 |
| Deductive vs deductive + consistency | 2 | 8 | 0.109 |
| Deductive + abductive vs complete | 2 | 0 | 0.500 |
| Deductive + consistency vs complete | 2 | 3 | 1.000 |

The 95% Wilson confidence intervals of all four exact-match rates overlap. They run from 86.8–93.9% for deductive only to 91.0–96.7% for deductive plus abductive.

**Why it matters.** Only abduction's gain over deduction is significant at the 5% level. The statements that the complete system "is not the most accurate configuration" and that it "trails" deductive plus abductive rest on a 2-case difference with p = 0.5. An examiner who checks this will see over-interpretation.

**What to do.**
- Report confidence intervals with the exact-match rates, and the McNemar results as a table. **[I can do]** I can add both to the generated evaluation tables, so they stay reproducible.
- Reword the comparison. Abduction significantly improves on deduction. The three model-based configurations are statistically indistinguishable on the held-out split. Their differences appear in the sensitivity sweeps, under controlled noise and missing data, rather than in overall accuracy.
- Make the same correction in the abstract, Section 4.6, Section 6.3 and Chapter 7.

### A3. Simulator and knowledge base share one model

**Status: done.** `tests/mismatch.py` adds mild and strong physics profiles, plus re-baselining from normal data. See [12](12_model_mismatch.md) and Section 4 `sec:mismatch`. Results: mild mismatch costs abduction about 4 points and deduction 10; strong mismatch collapses every mode to 26–40%, and re-baselining three thresholds restores about 83%.

**What is wrong.** The report acknowledges this limitation, but an examiner will press harder than the text does. The held-out split comes from the same simulator, with the same physics and the same fault mechanisms, as the calibration split the thresholds were checked against. A different seed changes the random draws but not the model. Perfect scores on single faults therefore show that the rules agree with the simulator. They do not show that the rules would work on a real row.

**Why it matters.** This is the most likely line of attack in a viva: "Your 100% single-fault accuracy is circular. Isn't it?"

**What to do.**
- **Add a model-mismatch experiment. [I can do]** Generate an extra held-out split with deliberately changed simulator physics: different coil conductance, airflow shortfall mix, server power, sensor noise and fan-control margin. Then report how far accuracy falls. This directly measures robustness to the model being wrong, and it is the strongest available answer short of real data.
- In Chapter 4, describe the held-out split as testing generalisation across random cases from the same model. Do not describe it as an independent test.
- Keep the limitation in the abstract, as it is now.

### B1. ExplainabilityRate is 100% by construction

**Status: done.** `tests/fidelity.pl` removes the cited observations and rediagnoses (necessity), with a random control. Results are in [13](13_explanation_fidelity.md) and Section 4 `sec:fidelity`: necessity is 95.7% for deduction and 100% for the model-based modes, and the control is 99.6–100%. Two weaknesses were found and reported: single-proof traces (UPS bypass, R29/R35), and uncited noise estimates.

The metric counts a case as explained if every reported label has a proof, a present symptom or a hit conflict, plus a plan. The system only ever reports a label for one of those reasons, so the rate cannot fall below 100%. An examiner will notice that it measures the system's structure, not explanation quality.

**What to do.** Say so in Section 4.5. Present ExplainabilityRate as confirming that every output is traceable, and supplement it with a measure that can fail. One option is **explanation fidelity**: remove the evidence an explanation cites and check that the diagnosis changes. Another is a small evaluation by an engineer, scoring a sample of traces. **[I can do]** I can implement and report the fidelity check.

### B2. PlanValidity is essentially label precision

**Status: done.** Stated in Section 4.5, the Chapter 6 criteria table and the limitations.

One plan is issued per label, and a plan counts as valid when its label is correct. So PlanValidity duplicates diagnostic precision, and says nothing about whether the plan's actions are right. **What to do.** State this in Section 4.5. Argue the plans' validity separately, from their sources and verification steps, and ideally by review from a practitioner.

### B6. No data-driven baseline

**Status: done.** `tests/baseline.py` (scikit-learn, for comparison only) trains a random forest and a decision tree on the raw readings: on the calibration split, and on 2550 extra simulated cases. Results are in [17](17_baseline_comparison.md) and Section 4 `sec:baseline`.
- Calibration-trained classifiers: 52–58%.
- With 2550 cases: best 89.4% held-out and 87.1% over five seeds, against 94.5% and 93.6% for D+A, a significant difference.
- Mild mismatch: about 50%, against 91%.
- Strong mismatch: 38.0% against 40.0%, not significant.
- The classifiers do better on borderline and high-noise cases, which is reported.

The literature review argues that data-centre diagnosis has been data-driven, and that this project offers explainability instead. An examiner may ask: "How does your system compare with a simple classifier on the same data?" Without a baseline, the trade-off between accuracy and explanation is asserted rather than shown. **What to do.** Add a simple baseline, such as a decision tree or random forest trained on the calibration split and tested on the held-out split, implemented outside the Prolog system and reported only for comparison. The framework forbids *replacing* the symbolic system with machine learning, not comparing against it. Mention its expected weakness: it needs labelled faults and gives no explanation.

### B7. The modelled row has no cooling redundancy

**Status: done.** Acknowledged in the scope (Section 1, citing Barroso et al., 2018, for N+1/2N) and in the Chapter 6 limitations, with what redundancy would change for detecting F1 and F2. Redundant units are now specific future work. A two-unit variant was not built.

Real data-centre rows are normally served by redundant cooling units, N+1 or 2N. With one unit, a fan failure overheats the whole row, which makes $F_1$ easy to detect. With redundancy, the same failure would cause milder, more local symptoms. **What to do.** Acknowledge this in the scope and limitations, and list redundant units as future work. A stronger option is a variant with two cooling units, but that is a substantial change.

## D. Questions to prepare for a viva

1. *Why Prolog rather than a rule engine or a machine-learning model?* Point to declarative and procedural readings, proof trees for explanation, meta-interpretation, and the framework's requirement.
2. *Your test data come from your own simulator. Why should we believe the results?* Prepare the A3 answer, ideally with the model-mismatch experiment.
3. *What does your system do that a classifier could not?* Explanation with competing hypotheses, sensor faults identified by physics rather than training data, and no need for labelled fault data. Have the baseline (B6) ready if possible.
4. *Why combine abduction and consistency-based diagnosis, if the combination is not more accurate?* Explain the complementary roles. Abduction is robust to noise. Consistency checking enforces physical relations and separates sensors from equipment. Point to the cases each catches alone, and to the A2 significance results.
5. *How did you choose your thresholds, and how sensitive are the results to them?* Engineering reasoning first, a calibration check, the held-out test, and the threshold sweep showing that failures occur only when a threshold enters normal scatter.
6. *What is the closed-world assumption, and where is it safe here?* It is used for deductive negation and the normal conclusion. It is deliberately not used for missing evidence in the graded modes.
7. *Explain how the system tells a biased top-inlet sensor from real recirculation.* Outlet stratification, fault models and masking. Walk through Listing 3.1.
8. *Walk me through one rule and its source.* Pick one, such as r24 or r43, and know its provenance.
9. *What would you need to deploy this in a real facility?* Measured validation, redundant cooling, time series, sourced thresholds, and integration with the monitoring system.
10. *What was the hardest bug, and what did it teach you?* The chilled-water signature, or the reproducibility bug.
